\clearpage\section*{Abstract}\label{abstract}

This thesis asks whether labor-migration restrictiveness changes the
skill composition of legal employment-related inflows, rather than only
their volume. The argument is that restrictive all-migrant-worker
reforms can filter lower-skilled candidates at the margin. This is
because lower-skilled candidates rely more heavily on the general
work-permit channel, while high-skilled candidates may still enter
through dedicated routes. The prediction is therefore compositional: the
high-skilled share may rise even if the absolute number of high-skilled
permits does not.

Combining Eurostat first-residence-permit statistics with the
DEMIG-QuantMig Migration Policy Database, the thesis estimates the
relationship between cumulative net restrictive policy change and the
high-skilled share of employment-related permits for 30 European
destinations between 2008 and 2020. The empirical design constructs a
magnitude-weighted policy-change measure, separates all-migrant-worker
reforms from reforms targeting skilled/high-skilled and low-skilled
workers, and compares aggregate destination-year models with an improved
denominator-weighted bilateral origin-destination-year model using
destination and origin-year fixed effects.

The aggregate country-year model returns no significant association,
which is consistent with origin-side push factors adding noise that the
aggregate design cannot absorb. The improved preferred bilateral
specification absorbs those origin-year pressures and controls for
policy changes targeted specifically at skilled/high-skilled and
low-skilled workers. It supports the compositional prediction: larger
cumulative net restrictive policy change for all-migrant-worker reforms
is associated with a higher skill ratio among employment-related
permits (beta = 0.003278, p = 0.006, N = 6,301; wild-cluster bootstrap
p = 0.028). The corresponding PPML estimate for the absolute
high-skilled permit count is positive but not statistically significant
at the five-percent level (beta = 0.019855, p = 0.069), while the
work-visa-only share-model narrowing gives beta = 0.006702 with p =
0.002. The
implication is that dual-track migration systems do not simply
substitute for general labor-migration rules. They also shape who
continues to enter when general rules become more or less restrictive.

\section{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

Over the past two decades, most European countries have redesigned their
legal-entry rules for non-EU workers with the explicit aim of becoming
more selective. These reforms have taken many forms. They include
salary-threshold changes and national implementation around the EU Blue
Card, points-based routes for highly educated migrants in the
Netherlands, employer-driven work-permit reforms in Sweden, the German
Skilled Immigration Act of 2020, the French Talent Passport scheme, and
several rounds of fine-tuning in the Czech Republic, Slovakia, and the
Baltic states. Although these reforms differ across countries, they are
linked by a common policy concern: advanced economies increasingly rely
on high-skilled migrants. The OECD's International Migration Outlook
2024 documents that 2023 set a historic record for permanent-type
immigration to the OECD area, while member states are also facing
widespread labor shortages and the onset of population ageing (OECD
2024).

High-skilled inflows matter because they contribute to innovation,
productivity, and fiscal sustainability, while European labor markets
face persistent shortages; existing evidence also finds small
native-wage effects (Hunt and Gauthier-Loiselle 2010; Peri 2012;
Dustmann and Frattini 2014; OECD 2024; Card 2009).

Against this background, the central concern of this thesis is whether
labor-migration restrictiveness is associated with the skill composition
of legal employment-related entry. The research question is therefore:

\emph{How is labor-migration policy restrictiveness associated with the
high-skilled share of employment-related first residence permits in
European destination countries between 2008 and 2020?}

This question is not simply about whether restrictive policy reduces the
number of incoming workers but how restrictions change the skill
composition of the permit pool. To answer this question, the empirical
strategy combines two harmonized, annual datasets.
Eurostat\textquotesingle s first residence-permit statistics report
permits issued for remunerated activities, broken down by reason, permit
duration, citizenship, destination, and year. The outcome is restricted
to permits valid for at least twelve months. The policy regressor is
built from the DEMIG-QuantMig Migration Policy Database (Schreier,
Skrabal and Czaika 2023).

The analysis proceeds in two stages. The baseline model is an aggregate
destination-year fixed-effects regression with clustered standard errors
at the destination level. The preferred specification then moves to a
bilateral origin-destination-year model with destination fixed effects
and origin-year fixed effects. This model also weights observations by
the total employment-permit denominator used to calculate the
high-skilled share.

The central argument is compositional: general labor-migration
restrictions may reduce lower-skilled entry more than high-skilled entry
when high-skilled candidates can use dedicated routes. The predicted
result is a higher high-skilled share among employment-related first
permits, without a necessary increase in total permit volume. The thesis
tests this prediction in aggregate and bilateral 2008--2020 panels.

The aggregate model provides a broad comparison, while the bilateral
model absorbs origin-year pressures and supplies the main empirical
test. The discussion and robustness checks then assess how conditional
that association remains.

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews
the literature on migrant self-selection, policy effectiveness, and
skill-selective policy design, with particular attention to instrument
heterogeneity. Section 3 sets out the theoretical framework, drawing on
the Roy model of self-selection and the migration-gravity framework with
multilateral resistance. Section 4 sketches the institutional background
of European labor-migration policy. Section 5 specifies the empirical strategy, and
Section 6 describes the data. Section 7 reports the
results. Section 8 presents the robustness, sensitivity, and diagnostic
checks. Section 9 discusses what the results can and cannot support.
Section 10 develops the policy implications of the bilateral weighted
result, and Section 11 concludes with the policy reading foreshadowed in
this introduction.

\section{2. Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

\subsection{2.1 Self-selection and the Roy
model}\label{self-selection-and-the-roy-model}

The theoretical starting point is Borjas (1990), who applies the Roy
model to international migration. In this framework, migrants choose a
destination by comparing the expected returns to their human capital
across different countries, after taking migration costs into account.
Immigration policy is part of these costs. When legal entry, settlement,
or long-run security become more demanding in one destination, migrants
who have other realistic destination options may choose to move
elsewhere.

The key implication of this is that the response to destination policy
depends on the migrants\textquotesingle{} outside options. It suggests
that high-skilled workers generally have more destination choices than
low-skilled workers, because their skills are more transferable and more
widely demanded. In principle, this makes high-skilled migration more
elastic to changes in destination policy: when one country becomes less
attractive or more difficult to enter, high-skilled workers may be
better able to choose another destination.

Grogger and Hanson (2011) provide the empirical counterpart to this
argument using bilateral data on migrant stocks by educational
attainment in OECD destinations. They find both positive selection and
positive sorting. Positive selection means that more-educated workers
are more likely to emigrate. Positive sorting means that, once they
emigrate, more-educated workers tend to choose destinations with higher
returns to skill. Later studies reach similar conclusions. Macaluso
(2022) surveys the post-2010 selection literature and reports that
empirical work consistently finds positive selection on education.
Schmidt, Kristen and Mühlau (2022) also document that first-generation
immigrants in 18 European countries are predominantly positively
selected on educational attainment relative to their origin populations.

\subsection{2.2 Policy effectiveness and channel
substitution}\label{policy-effectiveness-and-channel-substitution}

Czaika and de Haas (2013) argue that the effectiveness of immigration
policy should not be judged only by whether reforms reduce aggregate
migration. In their account, restrictive reforms often change the
channel of migration, legal versus family versus irregular, more than
its overall amount. A policy can be effective in the intended direction
by changing timing, route, legal category, or composition, even when
aggregate flows remain substantial. Channel substitution between legal
visa types is not directly testable in residence-permit data, because
the Eurostat extract reports first-permit counts within each reason
category but does not track individual migrants across categories.

\subsection{2.3 Skill-selective policy design and instrument
heterogeneity}\label{skill-selective-policy-design-and-instrument-heterogeneity}

Skill-selective migration policy is not a single policy instrument. It
is a set of design choices that affect who can enter, how quickly they
can enter, and under what conditions they can remain. These choices can
include education requirements, occupational lists, salary thresholds,
points systems, researcher or talent permits, employer sponsorship
rules, labor-market tests, quotas, and recognition of qualifications.

These tools do not work the same way. Some select migrants directly
through education, skills, or other human-capital characteristics.
Others leave the selection process to employers. Some limit the number
of permits available, while others make the process of hiring from
abroad more administratively demanding. This matters because
restrictiveness and selectivity are related, but not the same. A reform
can make entry more restrictive for migrant workers in general while
still preserving, or even expanding, routes for skilled workers. At the
same time, a route that is formally designed for skilled workers may
still be unattractive if the salary threshold is too high, processing is
slow, family rights are limited, or employer obligations are heavy.

For this reason, a single aggregate measure of restrictiveness can hide
important differences between policy instruments. The same numerical
policy change may reflect a tighter quota, a stricter work-permit
condition, a new points system, or a change in employer liability. These
reforms are unlikely to affect the high-skilled share in exactly the
same way.

Czaika and Parsons (2017) are important for this thesis because they
show that the effect of high-skilled migration policy depends on the
instrument being used. Their comparison of policy tools suggests that
points systems are more effective at attracting and selecting
high-skilled migrants than instruments such as job-offer requirements,
labor-market tests, or shortage lists. The broader lesson is not that
one tool is always better in every country. Rather, policy design
changes the margin through which migrants and employers respond. A
points system mainly changes selection criteria; a salary threshold
changes the minimum market value of an applicant; a labor-market test
affects recruitment frictions; and a quota affects the scale of entry.

Cerna (2016) adds a political and institutional perspective to this
argument. She shows that European high-skilled immigration policy has
evolved selectively rather than uniformly. Countries do not simply
become more open or more closed. Instead, they adjust routes,
thresholds, and rights in response to labor-market needs, political
constraints, and institutional legacies. This helps explain why
high-skilled policy in Europe often looks like a layered system:
EU-level frameworks coexist with national talent schemes, researcher
permits, shortage-list channels, salary rules, and employer-sponsored
permits.

The measurement literature points in the same direction. Rayp, Ruyssen
and Standaert (2017), Helbling et al. (2017), and de Haas, Natter and
Vezzoli's (2015) work on migration-policy change show that immigration
policy is multidimensional. Visible entry rules and less visible rules
on stay, rights, and integration do not necessarily move together.

Taken together, the existing literature shows why skill selectivity and
general restrictiveness should be analyzed jointly rather than treated
as separate topics. Selection models explain why policy costs may affect
different skill groups differently. Policy-effectiveness studies show
that legal rules can redirect migration rather than simply stop it. The
literature on skill-selective instruments shows that European states
often tighten general access while preserving specialized channels for
high-skilled workers. The remaining empirical question is therefore
whether this institutional pattern appears in annual permit data as a
change in the skill composition of employment-related entry.

\subsection{2.4 Positioning relative to previous
methods}\label{positioning-relative-to-previous-methods}

Earlier studies mainly use one of three designs. Country-year
fixed-effects models, exemplified by Mayda (2010) and Ortega and Peri
(2013), exploit within-country variation in a hand-coded
plus-or-minus-one policy index. Bilateral gravity-style models with
multilateral resistance, in the tradition of Bertoli and
Fernández-Huertas Moraga (2013) and Czaika and Parsons (2017), embed
binary policy-tool dummies in a random-utility-maximisation framework.
Measurement-focused work, including Rayp, Ruyssen and Standaert (2017)
and Helbling et al. (2017), instead builds synthetic country-level
policy indices for later empirical use. This thesis combines a
transparent aggregate comparison with a bilateral
origin-destination-year model that accounts for multilateral resistance
through origin-year fixed effects.

\subsection{2.5 Measurement of migration
policy}\label{measurement-of-migration-policy}

Migration-policy databases differ in coverage and detail. DEMIG TIMER
provides long historical event data, IMPIC provides a structured index
through 2010, and DEMIG-QuantMig records European policy events through
2020 with fields for area, tool, target group, direction, and magnitude.

For this thesis, DEMIG-QuantMig is the most suitable source because it
covers the post-2008 European reform period and retains reform magnitude
and target-group detail. These features make the Restrict construction
transparent and aligned with the ratio outcome.

The choice is methodological rather than a claim that other databases
are inferior for every purpose: IMPIC is valuable for structured
cross-country comparison, while DEMIG TIMER is valuable for long
historical change. The present analysis needs dated, magnitude-weighted
events through 2020.

Selection theory motivates the high-skilled share, policy-effectiveness
research motivates the focus on restrictiveness, and the
channel-substitution and gravity literatures motivate the bilateral
design with origin-year fixed effects.

\subsection{2.6 The gap addressed by this
thesis}\label{the-gap-addressed-by-this-thesis}

The literature leaves a specific applied gap: it does not establish
whether general labor-migration restrictiveness is associated with the
skill composition of legal employment-related entry in an annual,
post-2008 European bilateral panel.

Earlier studies leave this question open for several reasons. Some
measure policy as a binary plus-one or minus-one dummy without
accounting for reform magnitude. Others treat all migrant-policy reforms
as one broad category, regardless of which group is targeted. Some focus
on the aggregate volume of inflows rather than their internal skill
composition. Others estimate the relationship only at the
destination-year level, which does not account for origin-specific
variation. As a result, the compositional question has not been answered
in the post-2008 European setting at the origin-destination-year level
with magnitude-weighted policy events.

Eurostat supplies annual first permit counts by reason, duration,
citizenship, destination, and year, while DEMIG-QuantMig records policy
area, target group, tool, direction, and magnitude through 2020.
Together they permit a magnitude-weighted bilateral test that earlier
country-year, binary-policy, or pre-2010 designs could not provide.

The thesis closes this gap through five contributions. First, the data
linkage of Eurostat first-residence-permit statistics with
DEMIG-QuantMig events closes the data gap, allowing yearly
skill-composition outcomes to be matched to dated policy reforms.
Second, the signed, magnitude-weighted Restrict measure closes the
measurement gap left by binary policy coding, preserving reform-size
information. Third, the separation of all-migrant-worker reforms from
skilled/high-skilled and low-skilled target-group reforms closes the
alignment gap between the policy regressor and the ratio outcome.
Fourth, the denominator-weighted bilateral origin-destination-year model
with destination and origin-year fixed effects closes the granularity
gap left by destination-year designs. Fifth, the wild-cluster bootstrap
closes the few-cluster inference gap. The resulting estimates remain
conditional associations rather than causal effects.

The thesis is therefore positioned as an applied empirical contribution
rather than as a causal-identification paper. The bilateral
specification, magnitude weighting, and group separation make the test
sharper than what is available in earlier work. However, the resulting
estimate should still be interpreted as a conditional association, not
as a settled causal effect. This scope is preserved throughout the
analysis, the discussion, and the conclusion.

\section{3. Theoretical Framework}\label{theoretical-framework}

This section sets out the theoretical mechanisms through which
destination restrictiveness can shape the skill composition of migration
flows. The framework builds on two strands of the migration literature
discussed in Section 2: the Roy-model logic of migrant self-selection
(Borjas 1990) and the gravity-style model of bilateral migration choice
with multilateral resistance (Bertoli and Fernández-Huertas Moraga
2013). Together, these two strands lead to the testable prediction that
the empirical strategy in Section 5 then applies.

The Roy model treats migration as a utility-maximization problem. A
potential migrant from origin o evaluates each available destination d
by comparing the expected utility of moving there with the cost of doing
so. The utility from destination d depends on the wage premium for the
migrant's skill level and on access to the destination labor market. The
cost includes fixed migration costs, such as relocation expenses,
language barriers, and information frictions. It also includes a
destination-specific policy cost, which reflects the legal and
administrative difficulty of entry, settlement, and long-run rights.
Destination labor-migration policy therefore enters directly into the
cost vector that potential migrants face.

The key implication is that the response to destination policy depends
on the migrants\textquotesingle{} outside options. In the standard
Roy-model reading, these outside options are alternative destinations.
High-skilled workers usually face a wider set of destinations offering
high returns to their human capital. They are therefore, in principle,
more responsive to the policy of any single destination than low-skilled
workers. In the European labor-migration setting examined here, however,
the most relevant outside-option set is not only the set of other
destinations. It is also the set of alternative entry channels within
the same destination.

The Roy model alone is not enough for empirical work because migration
is a bilateral choice. A migrant chooses one destination from a wider
menu of possible destinations. The migration-gravity literature
formalizes this choice within a Random Utility Maximization framework.
Bertoli and Fernández-Huertas Moraga (2013) show that the share of
migrants from origin o choosing destination d depends not only on the
characteristics of the origin-destination corridor, but also on
multilateral resistance. This term captures the relative attractiveness
of all other available destinations. When destination d becomes more
restrictive, migrants who move away from d are diverted toward whichever
alternative destinations have become relatively more attractive. In
standard data, this multilateral resistance term is unobserved. In the
preferred bilateral specification in Section 5, it is absorbed
non-parametrically by origin-year fixed effects.

Combining the Roy and gravity frameworks produces the prediction that
motivates the empirical specification of this thesis. For European labor
migration, the relevant outside options are not only alternative
destinations, but also alternative entry channels within a destination.
In many European destinations, high-skilled workers can use dedicated
legal routes such as the EU Blue Card, intra-company transfer schemes,
researcher visas, and national talent passports, including the German
Skilled Immigration Act, the French Talent Passport, and the Dutch
Highly Skilled Migrant scheme. Lower-skilled workers are less likely to
qualify for these routes because they usually cannot meet the salary,
skill, or qualification thresholds. They therefore depend more heavily
on the general work-permit channel.

For this reason, a tightening of the general work-permit channel is
expected to affect lower-skilled workers more strongly than high-skilled
workers who can still use a dedicated route. This is the asymmetric
entry-channel elasticity argument that motivates the empirical strategy.
It is different from the standard cross-destination elasticity argument,
which would predict the opposite sign. If the only relevant outside
option were another destination, high-skilled workers would be expected
to leave a tightening destination more quickly than lower-skilled
workers, and the high-skilled share would fall. In the European setting,
however, dedicated high-skill channels operate alongside general
work-permit rules. This makes the within-destination entry-channel
interpretation central to the thesis.

The prediction is therefore joint and compositional rather than purely
volume based. The high-skilled share of employment-related first
residence permits (HSRate) is expected to rise. Total employment-related
permits are expected to weakly fall or stay flat. The absolute
high-skilled permit count is expected to move either weakly upward or
stay flat. Taken together, these three movements would be consistent
with the compositional channel.

\section{4. Institutional Background}\label{institutional-background}

European labor-migration policy is organized across two layers. The
first is the EU layer, which sets minimum standards and harmonizes
certain categories of legal entry. Important examples include the EU
Blue Card Directive 2009/50/EC, later recast as Directive (EU)
2021/1883; the Researchers Directive 2005/71/EC, later replaced by
Directive (EU) 2016/801; and the Single Permit Directive 2011/98/EU.
These EU-level rules are important for the structure of legal labor
migration, but they do not apply uniformly across all Member States.
Denmark and Ireland are not bound by the Blue Card Directive; the United
Kingdom also did not participate while it was an EU Member State. This
uneven coverage is one reason why the main high-skilled numerator in
this thesis does not rely on Blue Card permits. Blue Card counts are
included only in the broader high-skilled-outcome robustness check.

The EU-level framework should not, however, be understood as a single
European work-permit regime. During the period covered by this thesis,
the Blue Card Directive allowed Member States to retain parallel
national schemes for highly qualified workers. The Single Permit
Directive has a different function: it creates a single application
procedure and a common set of rights for third-country workers, while
explicitly preserving national powers over admission to domestic labor
markets. The EU framework therefore standardizes selected procedures and
rights, but substantive entry criteria and admission decisions continue
to vary nationally. This is why meaningful policy variation remains
possible within a shared European legal setting (Council of the European
Union 2009; European Parliament and Council 2011).

The second layer is the national layer. This is where most of the
variation in labor-migration restrictiveness is designed. Member States
retain substantial discretion over salary thresholds, language
requirements, labor-market tests, shortage-list rules, points systems,
employer obligations, family-reunification rights, and national
high-skilled permit schemes that operate alongside the Blue Card.

A second institutional feature is important for interpretation. Under
TFEU Article 21 and Directive 2004/38/EC, EU citizens generally have the
right to take up employment in another Member State without a national
work permit. Accession dates and temporary labor-market restrictions,
however, mean that this did not apply uniformly to every citizenship
pair in the fixed EU27-2020 composition throughout 2008--2020.
National labor-migration reforms should therefore affect this group less
directly than third-country nationals, but the EU27 aggregate is an
imperfect placebo rather than a clean untreated group.

\section{5. Empirical Strategy}\label{empirical-strategy}

This section defines the variables, estimands, regression specifications,
and identifying assumptions before Section 6 documents their empirical
implementation. The distinction is deliberate: Section 5 explains what
is estimated and why, whereas Section 6 explains how the Eurostat
outcomes, DEMIG-QuantMig policy measures, controls, and estimation
samples are constructed.

\subsection{5.1 Variable construction}\label{variable-construction}

For destination d, origin o, and year t, the main dependent variable is
the high-skilled share of employment-related first residence permits. In
the aggregate panel this is measured at destination-year level; in the
bilateral panel it is measured at origin-destination-year level. The
denominator is always the Eurostat employment-reason permit flow, so the
target parameter is compositional within legal employment-permit
issuance. It should not be interpreted as a model of the
destination\textquotesingle s total employment level. The key policy
variables are the one-year lag of the all-migrant-worker cumulative net
policy-change score and the one-year lag of the all-migrant-worker
annual policy-flow measure. Parallel high-skilled and low-skilled
target-group policy measures enter as controls, because these targeted
reforms can affect the numerator and denominator of HSRate more
directly. For the aggregate models, the outcome variables are
constructed once from Non-EU permit totals and separately from fixed
EU27 permit totals. The two samples are not pooled, so the aggregate
notation does not require a citizenship-group index.

The main outcome variable is the high-skilled share, HSRate. The
high-skilled permit count, which adds the Eurostat categories for highly
skilled workers and researchers, enters as the numerator of HSRate and
is also used as the count outcome in the supporting Poisson
Pseudo-Maximum-Likelihood (PPML) test in Section 7.3. The share outcome
divides that count by the broader employment-related first-permit flow
in the same cell:

\[ \operatorname{HSNumber}_{d,t} = \operatorname{HighSkilledWorkerPermits}_{d,t} + \operatorname{ResearcherPermits}_{d,t} \]

\[ \operatorname{HSRate}_{d,t} = \frac{\operatorname{HSNumber}_{d,t}}{\operatorname{EmploymentPermitFlow}_{d,t}} \]

For clarity, the control for the size of the permit channel is labelled
PermitScale. PermitScale is the logged lagged number of all
employment-related first residence permits in the relevant cell. In the
aggregate model this is the destination-year permit flow; in the
bilateral model it is the origin-destination-year permit flow.
PermitScale is the lagged log of the same employment-permit count that
appears in the HSRate denominator, but the two enter the equation at
different time periods (t minus 1 versus t) and different functional
forms (log versus reciprocal-implicit-in-the-ratio); PermitScale
therefore functions as a scale control in the migration-policy panel
tradition and does not duplicate the dependent
variable\textquotesingle s denominator. The label is deliberately chosen
to avoid the mistaken impression that the variable refers to total
employment in the destination labor market. The preferred bilateral
model also uses the current permit denominator, EMP, as an estimation
weight. This is not a second outcome variable. It is a precision
adjustment: a high-skilled share calculated from 5,000 employment
permits is less noisy than the same share calculated from 10 permits.

\[ \operatorname{PermitScale}_{d,t-1} = \log\!\left(1 + \operatorname{EmploymentPermitFlow}_{d,t-1}\right) \]

\subsection{5.2 Regression
specifications}\label{regression-specifications}

The three specifications below are presented in a fixed order, from the
simplest aggregate model to the preferred bilateral one. Equations (1)
and (2) each represent one common specification that is run separately
on two aggregate panels. The main run uses permit totals summed across
Non-EU citizenships. This is the policy-exposed sample because national
labor-migration entry rules principally govern third-country nationals.
The placebo run uses permit totals summed across the fixed EU27-2020
citizenship composition, excluding the destination country itself. It
applies the same policy regressors, macroeconomic controls, and fixed
effects, but reconstructs HSNumber, HSRate, and PermitScale from EU27
permit flows. Because EU citizens generally enter under free-movement
rights rather than the national rules measured by Restrict, the EU27
coefficient should be close to zero if the policy variable is isolating
its intended channel. Equation (1) uses the cumulative policy-change
score, while high-skilled and low-skilled target-group policy paths
enter as controls:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{HSRate}_{d,t} ={}& \alpha_d + \lambda_t
+ \beta\,\operatorname{RestrictAll}_{d,t-1}
+ \theta_{HS}\,\operatorname{RestrictHS}_{d,t-1} \\
&+ \theta_{LS}\,\operatorname{RestrictLS}_{d,t-1}
+ \gamma\,\operatorname{PermitScale}_{d,t-1} \\
&+ \delta\,\operatorname{Unemployment}_{d,t-1}
+ \varphi\,\operatorname{GDPGrowth}_{d,t-1}
+ \varepsilon_{d,t}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\end{equation*}

In Equation (1), d indexes destination countries and t indexes years.
There is no citizenship-group index because Non-EU and EU27 observations
are not stacked in one regression; the displayed equation is estimated
once for each separately constructed panel. Neither aggregate panel
contains individual-origin resolution: non-EU origins are collapsed
into one Non-EU total and EU27 origins into one EU27 total. Equation
(3), introduced later in this section, instead retains individual
origins and indexes them by o. Destination fixed effects are represented
by alpha, year fixed effects by lambda, and the coefficient of interest
is beta on RestrictAll, the lagged cumulative net policy-change score for
all-migrant-worker reforms. RestrictHS and RestrictLS are included as
policy controls because they capture reforms that directly target the
high-skilled numerator or the lower-skilled part of the denominator.
PermitScale controls for the size of the permit denominator from which
the high-skilled share is calculated; it does not measure aggregate
employment in the host economy.

PermitScale is included in each group-specific model run and is computed
from that sample\textquotesingle s own employment-permit flow. This is
intentional: the EU27 placebo should mirror the Non-EU specification as
closely as possible, so the citizenship sample is the only substantive
difference between the two aggregate estimations. Keeping the same
control structure makes their coefficients comparable.

Equation (2) follows the same separate Non-EU and EU27 estimation
procedure. It preserves the controls and fixed effects from Equation (1)
but replaces the cumulative policy stock with annual policy-flow
measures. It tests whether newly adopted all-migrant-worker reforms in a
given year are associated with the following year\textquotesingle s
permit composition, while again controlling for newly adopted
high-skilled and low-skilled target-group reforms:

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{HSRate}_{d,t} ={}& \alpha_d + \lambda_t
+ \beta\,\operatorname{PolicyFlowAll}_{d,t-1}
+ \theta_{HS}\,\operatorname{PolicyFlowHS}_{d,t-1} \\
&+ \theta_{LS}\,\operatorname{PolicyFlowLS}_{d,t-1}
+ \gamma\,\operatorname{PermitScale}_{d,t-1} \\
&+ \delta\,\operatorname{Unemployment}_{d,t-1}
+ \varphi\,\operatorname{GDPGrowth}_{d,t-1}
+ \varepsilon_{d,t}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\end{equation*}

The preferred specification is bilateral and denominator-weighted. It
estimates the high-skilled permit share for each origin-destination-year
corridor as a function of the lagged cumulative net policy-change score
for all-migrant-worker reforms, lagged cumulative policy-change scores
for skilled/high-skilled and low-skilled target groups, lagged bilateral
PermitScale, lagged destination unemployment, and lagged destination GDP
growth, combined with destination fixed effects and origin-year fixed
effects. The all-migrant-worker coefficient remains the coefficient of
interest. The skill-targeted policy controls are included because
high-skilled reforms can directly affect the numerator of HSRate, while
low-skilled reforms can affect the lower-skilled part of the
employment-permit denominator. The bilateral PermitScale control adjusts
for the previous year\textquotesingle s size of the origin-destination
permit corridor, while the estimation weight is the current total
employment-permit denominator used to calculate the dependent share.
Destination fixed effects absorb stable country differences, while
origin-year fixed effects absorb all shocks common to migrants from the
same origin in the same year, including origin labor-market conditions
and multilateral resistance to migration. This is the strongest design
available with the data at hand because it separates general
labor-migration restrictiveness from targeted skill-selective reforms,
absorbs origin-year migration pressures, and reduces noise from
small-denominator permit cells.

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{HSRate}_{o,d,t} ={}& \alpha_d + \mu_{o,t}
+ \beta\,\operatorname{RestrictAll}_{d,t-1}
+ \theta_{HS}\,\operatorname{RestrictHS}_{d,t-1} \\
&+ \theta_{LS}\,\operatorname{RestrictLS}_{d,t-1}
+ \gamma\,\operatorname{PermitScale}_{o,d,t-1} \\
&+ \delta\,\operatorname{Unemployment}_{d,t-1}
+ \varphi\,\operatorname{GDPGrowth}_{d,t-1}
+ \varepsilon_{o,d,t}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\end{equation*}

In Equation (3), o indexes origin countries and mu represents
origin-year fixed effects. RestrictAll is the cumulative net
policy-change score for all-migrant-worker reforms and remains the
coefficient of interest. RestrictHS and RestrictLS are parallel
cumulative scores for reforms targeting skilled/high-skilled workers and
low-skilled workers. They enter as controls so that \(\beta\) is
interpreted as the association of general employment-migration
restrictiveness with HSRate, holding targeted skill-selective policy
paths constant. The preferred estimate is obtained by weighted least
squares with weights w\_odt = EMP\_odt, where EMP\_odt is the total
number of employment-related permits in the origin-destination-year
cell. The target parameter is therefore a permit-denominator-weighted
association rather than an equal-cell association. The design is
preferred because origin-year fixed effects absorb all shocks that
affect migrants from the same origin in the same year, including
origin-side labor-market conditions and multilateral resistance to
migration, while the weights account for the fact that the dependent
share is measured with different precision across cells.

A companion count-outcome specification addresses the volume side of the
question. It is a Poisson Pseudo-Maximum-Likelihood (PPML) test on the
same bilateral panel, following Santos Silva and Tenreyro (2006). The
PPML specification estimates the count outcome as a function of the
all-migrant-worker Restrict measure, the skilled/high-skilled and
low-skilled target-group policy controls, destination macro controls,
destination fixed effects, and origin-year fixed effects, with standard
errors clustered by destination. The coefficient is interpreted as the
percent change in the count outcome per one-unit change in the
all-migrant-worker Restrict measure. PPML is useful here because it
handles zero counts in the bilateral panel without the log-of-zero
problem that would affect an OLS-on-log specification.

Taken together, these three equations distinguish the aggregate share
model, the policy-flow alternative, and the bilateral origin-year
fixed-effects model on the same share outcome (HSRate). The empirical
results are reported only after the identification logic, set out in the
following section, has been introduced.

\subsection{5.3 Identification and
inference}\label{identification-and-inference}

The main identification problem is that migration policy is endogenous
to economic conditions. The empirical strategy addresses this problem
only partially. Destination fixed effects absorb stable country
differences, such as long-standing institutional quality, language,
geography, administrative capacity, or persistent openness to
immigration. Year fixed effects in the aggregate model absorb common
European shocks. Origin-year fixed effects in the preferred bilateral
model absorb all shocks that affect migrants from the same origin in the
same year, including origin-country economic crises, conflicts, and the
changing attractiveness of alternative destinations. Lagged unemployment
and lagged GDP growth control for broad destination economic conditions
before the outcome year, and the one-year lag on policy reduces direct
simultaneity between a policy event and permits issued in the same year.
A separate identification concern is that European destinations operate
under common EU labor-migration directives. Origin-year fixed effects
absorb the component of an EU-level shock that is common to every
sampled destination for a given origin and year. They do not by
themselves absorb differences created by non-EU destinations, national
opt-outs, or Croatia's 2013 accession. The Restrict coefficient is
identified from differential national policy variation within each
origin-year, and Robustness 9 additionally controls for the directive
windows that applied to participating destinations.

The separately estimated EU27 model described above is interpreted as a
limited falsification check, not as a second treated sample. Because EU
citizens generally have free-movement rights, national labor-migration
restrictions aimed at third-country workers should not directly affect
their legal entry in the same way. The expected EU27 coefficient is
therefore close to zero. A statistically significant coefficient with
the same sign as the Non-EU coefficient would instead warn that Restrict
may be proxying for destination-wide shocks that influence both permit
series. The fixed EU27-2020 composition, accession timing, transitional
restrictions, sparse EU permit reporting, and the fact that free movers
generally do not require residence permits make this placebo informative
but imperfect rather than a clean untreated comparison group.

Stricter causal alternatives were considered but are not used as the
main design. A conventional difference-in-differences or event-study
design would require a small number of clearly timed comparable reforms
and a credible untreated comparison group. The DEMIG-QuantMig data
instead contain many overlapping reforms with different magnitudes,
tools, and target groups, often within the same country-year, so forcing
them into a single treatment date would discard important policy
variation. An instrumental-variable design would require a source of
variation that shifts labor-migration restrictiveness without directly
affecting the skill composition of permit inflows; no credible
harmonized instrument is available for the full European panel. The
fixed-effects design is therefore chosen not because it proves
causality, but because it is the most transparent way to use the annual
policy-event variation while making the identifying assumptions
explicit.

Standard errors are clustered by destination country. The number of
clusters is about 30, so the thesis treats marginal results cautiously
and does not describe 10-percent findings as main evidence. In the
results, the main bilateral table uses a conservative fixed-effect-aware
finite-sample correction, counting the absorbed destination and
origin-year fixed effects in the effective parameter count. The caution
is consistent with the cluster-robust inference literature, where
few-cluster settings require especially careful interpretation (Liang
and Zeger 1986; Cameron, Gelbach and Miller 2008; MacKinnon and Webb
2018).

To address the few-cluster concern directly, a wild-cluster bootstrap
following Cameron, Gelbach and Miller (2008) is implemented for the
denominator-weighted preferred model. The procedure imposes the null of
\(\beta\) = 0, draws Rademacher weights at the destination-cluster
level, constructs bootstrap outcomes, and re-estimates the full weighted
model in each replication. With B = 2,000 replications, the improved
weighted preferred estimate remains significant at the five-percent
level: the cluster-robust t-statistic is 3.00 and the bootstrap
two-sided p-value is 0.028.

The all-migrant-worker-only policy definition addresses a mechanical
concern that would otherwise be present. When the dependent variable is
a high-skilled share, policies aimed specifically at skilled or
high-skilled workers can directly target the numerator, while policies
aimed at low-skilled workers can affect the lower-skilled part of the
denominator. The cleanest interpretation of general policy changes
affecting the full labor-migration denominator therefore comes from
keeping the all-migrant-worker-only score as the focal policy measure
and adding the skilled/high-skilled and low-skilled target-group
measures as controls.

With the estimating equations and identifying assumptions established,
the next section documents the data sources, operational definitions,
and sample filters used to implement them.

\section{6. Data}\label{data}

\subsection{6.1 Outcome data: Eurostat residence-permit
statistics}\label{outcome-data-eurostat-residence-permit-statistics}

The outcome data come from Eurostat's harmonized residence-permit
statistics on first permits issued for remunerated activities. The
dataset reports annual permit counts by reason for entry, length of
validity, citizenship, destination country, and year. The analysis
covers the period from 2008 to 2020, keeps observations measured as
persons, and restricts the sample to permits valid for at least twelve
months. The 2020 endpoint is retained because it is the final year
covered by the policy database. Because the COVID-19 pandemic could
disrupt permit issuance in that year, the robustness checks also report
a model that excludes 2020.

The main outcome is built from the employment-related permit categories.
The denominator is the broad employment-permit category, while the
high-skilled numerator combines permits issued to highly skilled workers
and researchers. EU Blue Card permits are not included in the main
high-skilled numerator. They are used only in a robustness check with a
broader high-skilled definition.

It is important to clarify what ``employment'' means in this thesis. The
term refers to Eurostat's permit-reason category for first permits
issued for employment or remunerated-activity reasons. It does not refer
to labor-market employment in the destination economy, total employment
in the economy, or a stock of employed people. The employment variable
is therefore a permit-flow denominator: the number of first
employment-reason permits issued to the relevant citizenship group in a
destination and year. The high-skilled numerator is measured within the
same permit universe and counts the highly skilled worker and researcher
permit categories. Because Eurostat reports annual counts by permit
reason rather than individual permit histories, it cannot identify
whether the same migrant later changes permit category; the analysis
therefore measures the skill composition of first employment-related
permits rather than substitution between legal channels. This means a
worker entering on a highly skilled worker permit or a researcher permit
is therefore counted in the high-skilled numerator, regardless of the
job performed after arrival. By contrast, a worker entering on a general
employment-related permit is counted only in the denominator, even if
that worker later moves into a high-skilled role.

This first-permit logic is treated as a feature of the research design
rather than as a flaw. The policy levers studied in this thesis operate
precisely at the point of first admission. The first-permit
classification is therefore the margin most directly affected by the
policy changes recorded in DEMIG-QuantMig.

The main dependent variable is the high-skilled share of
employment-related first residence permits. It is calculated as
high-skilled permits divided by all employment-related permits. This
means that the outcome is not the high-skilled share of all workers in
the destination economy. The corresponding count outcome, used in the
supporting Poisson Pseudo-Maximum Likelihood volume test in Section 7.3,
is the number of high-skilled permits, labelled HSNumber. The current
Eurostat export does not include ready-made non-EU and EU27 citizenship
aggregates. For this reason, the code manually sums the relevant
individual citizenship rows. This is disclosed as an explicit data
limitation.

\subsection{6.2 Policy regressor:
DEMIG-QuantMig}\label{policy-regressor-demig-quantmig}

The restrictiveness measure used in this thesis is not a ready-made
numerical score supplied in the dataset. It is constructed from the
event-level DEMIG-QuantMig Migration Policy Database (Schreier, Skrabal,
and Czaika 2023), which records dated migration-policy reforms rather
than legal-stock snapshots. The local export used for replication
contains 7,643 rows for 31 European countries, with dated policy records
running from 1989 through 2020. Each row is one recorded policy event,
with fields for destination country, year, policy area, policy tool,
target group, target origin, restrictiveness direction, and magnitude.
The thesis then filters these records to match the research design
described in the next paragraphs.

DEMIG-QuantMig is used because it records policy changes rather than
only describing the legal situation at one point in time. For each
reform, the database identifies the destination country and year, the
policy field, the instrument affected, the migrant group targeted,
whether the reform made policy more or less restrictive, and how large
the change was. This structure makes it possible to build a country-year
measure of policy tightening or liberalization that can be matched to
Eurostat\textquotesingle s annual permit-flow outcomes. The Restrict
policy-change measure used in this thesis multiplies the signed
direction of each event by its magnitude weight, giving fine-tuning
reforms a weight of one and major reforms a weight of four.

The construction proceeds in four steps. First, only events between 2007
and 2020 in the policy area Legal entry and stay are retained, because
the outcome is based on first residence permits issued for
employment-related entry and stay rather than asylum, border control,
exit, or integration policy. Second, only events targeting the relevant
migrant group enter each measure. The analysis distinguishes three
policy definitions: the skilled/high-skilled-targeted measure, the
low-skilled-targeted measure, and the all-migrant-worker-only measure.
The all-migrant-worker-only definition remains the coefficient of
interest in the improved preferred model, while the skilled/high-skilled
and low-skilled definitions enter as policy controls.

These two target-group measures are included to avoid mixing the main
policy channel with reforms that directly affect one side of the ratio.
The skilled/high-skilled control captures changes aimed specifically at
skilled or high-skilled migrants, which may directly affect the
high-skilled numerator. The low-skilled control captures changes aimed
specifically at lower-skilled migrants, which may affect the
lower-skilled part of the denominator. They are therefore adjustment
variables; the main coefficient remains the all-migrant-worker
policy-change score.

The remaining two steps translate retained events into a numerical
score. Each event is converted into a signed magnitude, where direction
is coded as More restrictive = +1, Less restrictive = -1, and No change
= 0, and where magnitude is coded as Fine-tuning = 1, Minor = 2,
Mid-level = 3, and Major = 4. Events with N/A magnitude are retained
with weight 1, so that an assessable restrictiveness change is not
dropped only because its size is not separately graded. Finally, the
event score is the product of direction and magnitude, annual
policy-flow scores are summed within each country-year, and the
cumulative net policy-change score is the running country-year sum of
those annual scores.

Formally, let i index a recorded DEMIG-QuantMig event with assigned
direction \(d_{i}\) in \{+1, 0, -1\}, where +1 = More restrictive, 0 =
No change, and -1 = Less restrictive, and magnitude weight \(m_{i}\) in
\{1, 2, 3, 4\}. Events with N/A magnitude are retained with weight 1 so
that an assessable restrictiveness change is not dropped only because
its size is not separately graded. The signed event score is the product
of direction and magnitude:

\textbf{Magnitude weight assignment used to build the Restrict measure:}

\[ e_i = d_i \cdot m_i \]

For destination d and year t, the annual policy-flow score is the sum of
event scores over all retained events that occur in that
destination-year:

\[ \operatorname{flow}_{d,t} = \sum_{i \in E(d,t)} e_i \]

The cumulative net policy-change score, denoted Restrict, is the running
country-year sum of the annual policy-flow scores from the 2007 baseline
through year t. Although permit outcomes are observed from 2008 to 2020,
the policy-score panel begins in 2007. This extra year establishes the
one-year policy lag for the first observed outcome year. The cumulative
score is initialized at zero before 2007. The fully controlled aggregate
and bilateral regressions nevertheless begin in 2009, because
Eurostat\textquotesingle s first-permit series begins in 2008 and cannot
supply the lagged PermitScale required for a 2008 full-control
observation.

\[ \operatorname{Restrict}_{d,t} = \sum_{s=2007}^{t}\operatorname{flow}_{d,s} \]

The regression regressor used in the empirical specifications is the
one-year lag of this cumulative score, Restrict\textsubscript{d,t-1},
and the annual-flow regressor is the one-year lag of the annual
policy-flow score, flow\textsubscript{d,t-1}. By construction, positive
values of Restrict\textsubscript{d,t} indicate that the retained policy
changes through year t are net restrictive, and negative values indicate
net liberalizing. A value of zero corresponds to either no change since
the baseline or restrictive and liberalizing changes that exactly offset
each other in magnitude-weighted terms.

\subsection{6.3 Controls and sample
construction}\label{controls-and-sample-construction}

The destination-year controls are lagged unemployment and lagged real
GDP growth. GDP growth is complete in the current panel, while
unemployment has 37 missing country-year values and therefore reduces
the full-control aggregate sample.

The empirical base for the analysis is sizeable. After the main Eurostat
filters are applied, the residence-permit data contain 305,448
long-format rows before aggregation and 1,274 constructed outcome rows
across citizenship groups. The DEMIG-QuantMig database covers more than
7,600 policy changes. The density of official permit data and documented
policy events makes the measurement basis relatively reliable and allows
several robustness checks. A large sample alone, however, cannot resolve
policy endogeneity, which is why careful controls and cautious
interpretation remain necessary throughout.

These controls are included because destination-country economic
conditions can shape both immigration policy and immigrant inflows. A
government facing high unemployment or weak growth may be more likely to
tighten work-permit conditions, expand labor-market tests, or impose
stricter employer obligations. Conversely, a government facing strong
growth, labor shortages, or demographic pressure may liberalize entry
routes or design rules that favor workers with scarce skills. The same
conditions can also affect the number and composition of permits issued.
Lagged unemployment and lagged GDP growth therefore help control for the
economic environment in which policy decisions are made, although they
remain broad proxies rather than a full measure of labor demand,
vacancies, wages, or sector-specific shortages.

The revised design also uses origin-year fixed effects in the bilateral
model. Origin-year fixed effects absorb those time-varying origin
conditions non-parametrically, which is preferable to adding incomplete
controls that would make the model look more detailed without measuring
origin-side conditions consistently.

\section{7. Results}\label{results}

\subsection{7.1 Aggregate country-year
results}\label{aggregate-country-year-results}

The aggregate destination-year models are reported first because they
show what the data look like before origin-side pressures are absorbed.
In this design, the compositional association is not visible. That is
consistent with the Section 3 logic: high-skilled migration decisions
depend on origin conditions, alternative destinations, networks, and
labor-market opportunities that a destination-year specification cannot
capture. Those omitted origin-side factors enter the aggregate residual
and can swamp the policy signal. With the full set of destination
controls added to country and year fixed effects, the current
all-migrant-worker cumulative coefficient is \(\beta = \ 0.001180\ \)
with cluster-robust SE = 0.001843 (t = 0.64, p = 0.527, N = 308, G =
30). The annual policy-flow alternative is also not significant
(\(\beta = \ 0.001339\), SE = 0.002060, p = 0.521), and the aggregate
log high-skilled-count outcome is not significant either
(\(\beta = \ 0.021726\), SE = 0.020393, p = 0.296). The aggregate
evidence therefore offers no support for a compositional or volume
response under the cleaner all-migrant-worker policy definition with
skill-target controls; the preferred test is the bilateral specification
in Section 7.2.

\subsection{7.2 Preferred bilateral origin-year fixed-effects

keeps the cleaner policy definition, absorbs origin-year shocks, and

depend on origin conditions, alternative destinations, networks, and

controls, while the dependent ratio is noisier in very small permit

is therefore not a claim about short-run annual policy events; it is a
cumulative net-policy-change association estimated with
denominator-aware bilateral variation.

\subsection{7.3 Interpretation of the significant and non-significant
findings}\label{interpretation-of-the-significant-and-non-significant-findings}

The results point to a composition association, not a volume effect. The
high-skilled share is positively associated with the cumulative net
policy-change measure in the improved preferred denominator-weighted
bilateral specification ($\beta$ = 0.003278, p = 0.006, N = 6,301; bootstrap p
= 0.028), consistent with the joint prediction stated in Section 3. The
aggregate all-migrant-worker cumulative and annual-flow models are not
significant, the total employment-permit denominator is essentially flat
in both the aggregate and bilateral panels, and the bilateral
high-skilled permit count is positive in the PPML comparison ($\beta$ =
0.0199) but not significant at the five-percent level. The evidence
therefore concerns the relative skill mix of employment-related permits
rather than a robust increase in the absolute number of high-skilled
entries.

The PPML statistical detail is as follows. The high-skilled count
returns a semi-elasticity of \(\beta =\) 0.0199 (SE = 0.0105, p = 0.069,
N = 6,301, G = 30) after adding the same high-skilled and low-skilled
target-group policy controls used in the improved preferred share model.
PPML on the total employment-permit count returns \(\beta =\) -0.0008
(SE = 0.0207, p = 0.969). The high-skilled count is therefore positive
but only marginal at the 10-percent level, while the total
employment-permit denominator is essentially flat. The difference
between the two semi-elasticities implies a composition shift of roughly
2 percent per Restrict unit, close to the OLS-weighted share result of
about 1.6 percent of the mean. The two estimators therefore point in the
same direction: composition shifts upward, while neither absolute count
moves significantly at the five-percent level. The significant HSRate
result therefore identifies a relative shift in permit composition, but
it cannot separately establish whether that shift reflects fewer
lower-skilled permits, more high-skilled permits, or both. This pattern
is consistent with the entry-channel-elasticity mechanism, but it does
not directly establish the separate permit-count responses underlying
the compositional shift.

This interpretation is also consistent with the contrast between
aggregate and bilateral specifications. Under the synchronized design,
the aggregate cumulative all-migrant-worker model with high-skilled and
low-skilled policy controls is not significant (\(\beta =\) 0.001180, p
= 0.527, N = 308), and the aggregate annual-flow version is also not
significant (\(\beta =\) 0.001339, p = 0.521, N = 308). The improved
denominator-weighted bilateral model, however, remains significant after
controlling for skilled/high-skilled and low-skilled target-group policy
paths (\(\beta =\) 0.003278, p = 0.006, N = 6,301). The substantive
story is therefore narrower and more precise than a simple aggregate
reading would suggest. General restrictions are associated with
composition when the specification absorbs origin-year migration
pressures, accounts for noisy small-denominator cells, and separates
broad all-migrant-worker reforms from targeted skill-selective reforms.

Taken together, the strongest evidence appears when the model uses
bilateral variation, controls for origin-year pressures and
skill-targeted policy paths, and weights observations by the permit
denominator used to construct the share. By contrast, the aggregate
models provide weaker evidence, and the absolute-number models do not
show a consistent increase in high-skilled permits at the five-percent
level. This pattern is substantively important: it suggests that the
cumulative net policy-change measure for all-migrant-worker reforms is
more clearly related to the relative skill mix of employment-related
permits than to the total volume of high-skilled entries.

Robustness 1 (all-first-permits denominator) tests whether the preferred
result depends on using the narrower employment-permit denominator. It
keeps the numerator as highly skilled worker plus researcher permits but
divides it by the full first-residence-permit pool for the same
origin-destination-year cell: family, education, employment, and other
reasons. Under the improved policy specification with skill-target
controls, the preferred-style weighted bilateral coefficient remains
positive but is marginal rather than conventionally significant
(\(\beta =\) 0.000574, p = 0.054, N = 5,392). The corridor-FE variant is
similarly marginal (\(\beta =\) 0.000480, p = 0.054), while the version
that keeps the original employment-permit scale control remains
significant (\(\beta =\) 0.000632, p = 0.028, N = 6,301). This supports
the interpretation that the association is not only a mechanical feature
of the employment-permit denominator, but the check remains secondary
because the broader denominator includes permit channels that
labor-migration policy does not directly target.

Robustness 2 (core labor-entry subset) asks whether the result depends
on the exact set of DEMIG-QuantMig policy tools retained in the
preferred Restrict measure. The core labor-entry subset retains only the
policy tools that directly affect the employer-permit entry decision:
work visa/permit rules, quota/target, entry visa/stay permit, employer
liabilities, and other sanctions tied to non-compliance. It excludes
free mobility rights or agreements, action plans and strategy reports,
integration programs, and general access-to-rights categories. These
excluded tools are part of the DEMIG-QuantMig Legal entry and stay area
but do not directly govern who is admitted under the general work-permit
channel. After adding the high-skilled and low-skilled target-group
policy controls, the core-subset re-estimation remains positive and
close to the preferred estimate but is only marginal at the ten-percent
level (\(\beta =\) 0.003003, p = 0.069). The check therefore supports
the direction of the preferred result, but it should not be described as
an additional five-percent significant result.

A further aggregate outcome check adds EU Blue Card permits to the
high-skilled numerator while retaining the skill-target policy controls.
The resulting coefficient is negative and not statistically significant
($\beta$ = -0.002279, p = 0.367). This result does not provide aggregate
support for the association when the numerator is broadened to include
Blue Card permits. It remains a secondary check, however, because Blue
Card coverage is not uniform across the sample and the specification is
an aggregate destination-year model rather than the preferred
denominator-weighted bilateral design.

Panel B of Table 8 contains the placebo and long-difference diagnostics.
Rather than simply altering a control or outcome definition, they ask
whether the estimated relationship appears where the identification
logic predicts it should appear and disappears where it should not.

Robustness 3 (EU27 cumulative placebo) does not provide positive
falsification support. Its coefficient is positive and marginal at the
ten-percent level (0.011629, p = 0.086), whereas the aggregate Non-EU
counterpart is small and imprecise (M3: 0.001180, p = 0.527). Because
the EU27 estimate is based on only 38 observations and 13 destination
clusters, it is too imprecise to establish a clear alternative
explanation by itself. Its same-signed direction is nevertheless
consistent with the policy measure capturing destination-wide shocks
that affect both EU and Non-EU permit composition. The fixed EU27-2020
composition also spans Croatia's accession and temporary labor-market
restrictions, so it is not a clean untreated group. This aggregate
placebo does not directly invalidate the preferred bilateral estimate,
but it makes the falsification evidence cautionary and inconclusive
rather than reassuring.

Robustness 4 (policy-flow placebo and long-difference) is also cautious.
The EU27 policy-flow placebo is positive and marginal at the ten-percent
level (\(\beta =\) 0.010176, p = 0.076), again on only 38 observations
and 13 clusters. The long-difference check is negative and not
significant (\(\beta =\) -0.007443, p = 0.342, N = 31). For this reason,
the thesis treats the EU27 placebo and the long-difference exercise as
diagnostics rather than as strong identification tests. A bilateral
version of the EU27 placebo, which would have been the closest mirror of
the preferred denominator-weighted bilateral design, is not run because
EU-origin residence-permit cells are too sparse and selective to provide
a comparable bilateral outcome: EU free movers generally do not require
the permits measured by migr\_resocc. Although the raw extract contains
individual EU citizenship rows, treating those permit observations as a
bilateral migration-flow panel would not mirror the Non-EU outcome. The
aggregate EU27 check is therefore retained only as a limited diagnostic.

Robustness 5 (fractional-response) was added because the dependent
variable is a share bounded between zero and one. Following the
fractional-response logic of Papke and Wooldridge, the current
controlled model uses a quasibinomial logit specification weighted by
the permit denominator and clustered by destination. It produces a
positive but marginal aggregate estimate for the all-migrant-worker
cumulative measure (\(\beta =\) 0.046665, p = 0.082). It is therefore
not used as the main model. The preferred approach keeps a linear share
model because its coefficient is directly interpretable in
percentage-point terms but uses denominator weights to address the most
important measurement issue created by small permit cells.

Robustness 6 replaces Eurostat unemployment with World Bank indicator
SL.UEM.TOTL.ZS, the modeled ILO unemployment estimate sourced from
ILOSTAT (World Bank 2026), and estimates no-UK and UK-inclusive variants. The main
Eurostat sample excludes the United Kingdom because the Eurostat control
extract used here contains no UK unemployment observations for the
estimation window; Brexit is also not fully represented as a single
change in the cumulative policy score.

The weighted preferred result is robust to replacing Eurostat
unemployment with ILOSTAT when the United Kingdom remains excluded. The
Eurostat no-UK weighted estimate is \(\beta =\) 0.003278 with SE =
0.001094 (p = 0.006, N = 6,301, G = 30). The ILOSTAT no-UK weighted
estimate is \(\beta =\) 0.003006 with SE = 0.001074 (p = 0.009, N =
6,655, G = 30). The point estimate is slightly smaller but remains
significant at the five-percent level. When the United Kingdom is added
through ILOSTAT, however, the coefficient falls to \(\beta =\) 0.001359
with SE = 0.001817 (p = 0.460, N = 7,331, G = 31). This supports the
same interpretation as before: the main weighted result is not driven by
the Eurostat unemployment source, but it is conditional on excluding a
destination whose post-2016 migration-policy regime is not well
represented by the cumulative net policy-change measure.

The UK exercise is a limitation rather than proof that UK inclusion is
inappropriate: the preferred result is conditional on the no-UK sample,
and the Brexit-era measurement mismatch is a plausible explanation for
the difference.

Robustness 7 examines heterogeneity by origin income using the World
Bank FY2020 income classification, published in 2019 and based on 2018
Atlas-method GNI per capita (World Bank Data Team 2019). Under the
improved controlled model, the association remains positive and
significant for high-income origins (\(\beta =\) 0.009579, p = 0.011),
upper-middle-income origins (\(\beta =\) 0.002533, p = 0.017), and
lower-middle-income origins (\(\beta =\) 0.003759, p = 0.006). The
low-income-origin estimate is negative and statistically significant
(\(\beta = \ \)-0.002484, p = 0.024), but this stratum is smaller (N =
382, G = 19) and should be
interpreted cautiously. Overall, the income-stratified results are
heterogeneous rather than a uniform confirmation of the
entry-channel-elasticity mechanism: the association is positive for
high-, upper-middle-, and lower-middle-income origins but reverses sign
for low-income origins. This pattern may reflect differences in access
to dedicated routes, credential recognition, financial constraints, or
the composition of origin-destination corridors, none of which is
measured directly in the present design. The heterogeneity analysis
should therefore be read as qualifying the proposed mechanism and
identifying a direction for future research, rather than as a direct
test of why the composition response differs across origin-income
groups.

Robustness 8 isolates the single most-targeted policy instrument. The
preferred Restrict measure sums signed magnitude-weighted events across
all policy tools retained in the Legal entry and stay area. The
work-visa-only Restrict measure (Restrict\_WorkVisa\_lag1) instead uses
only the Work visa/permit tool, which is the instrument most directly
governing the general work-permit channel through which a lower-skilled
candidate would typically enter. Because this tool directly governs the
general work-permit channel, it provides a focused, though not
isolating, robustness check of the proposed mechanism. The preferred
bilateral weighted specification is re-estimated on the same N = 6,301
cells and G = 30 clustered destinations, with destination fixed effects,
origin-year fixed effects, the skill-targeted policy controls, the full
lagged economic control set, and the employment-permit denominator
weight. The work-visa-only coefficient is \(\beta =\) 0.006702 with
cluster-robust SE = 0.001942 (t = 3.45, cluster-robust p = 0.002). The
coefficient is approximately twice as large as the all-tools improved
preferred estimate of 0.003278. This pattern is consistent with the
proposed mechanism: when the restrictiveness measure is narrowed to the
tool most directly governing the general work-permit track, the
estimated composition association is larger. It should not be read as a
clean instrument-level test, however. Work visa/permit is also a common
tool among skilled/high-skilled-targeted reforms, so changes in this
category can be correlated with changes that directly affect
high-skilled entry. The skill-targeted policy controls partly account
for that overlap, but they cannot fully isolate the work-visa channel.
The work-visa-only result should therefore be read as corroborating the
direction of the proposed mechanism rather than uniquely identifying it.
It remains a robustness check because the all-tools preferred measure is
the more conservative summary of overall labor-migration restrictiveness
and matches the policy-area definition stated in Section 6.2.

Robustness 9 addresses a related concern that European destinations
operate under common EU labor-migration directives. If an EU-level
reform happened to coincide with national reforms recorded in
DEMIG-QuantMig, the Restrict coefficient could partly capture the
supranational shock. Origin-year fixed effects absorb only the component
common to every destination for a given origin-year; they do not absorb
different exposure caused by non-EU destinations, opt-outs, or accession
timing. The improved preferred weighted specification is therefore
re-estimated with controls for the transposition
windows of the four major EU labor-migration directives during the panel
period: the Blue Card Directive 2009/50/EC (window 2009-2011), the
Single Permit Directive 2011/98/EU (window 2011-2013), the
Intra-Corporate Transferee Directive 2014/66/EU (window 2014-2016), and
the Students and Researchers Directive 2016/801/EU (window 2016-2018).
The dummies are activated only for participating EU destinations in the
relevant year: Croatia enters from 2013, while Denmark and Ireland are
excluded because they do not participate in these directives. A single
combined applicable-window indicator gives \(\beta =\) 0.003359 with SE
= 0.001144 (p = 0.006). A more demanding variant adds the four
directive-specific dummies separately, which gives \(\beta =\) 0.003299
with SE = 0.001109 (p = 0.006). The Restrict coefficient remains very
close to the improved preferred estimate of 0.003278 in both variants.
None of the four directive dummies is statistically significant on its
own in the corrected specification. The stability of Restrict therefore
shows that the preferred association is not explained by these measured
directive windows, although the window controls are only coarse timing
proxies rather than reform-specific causal estimates. Graph 3 below
visualizes both layers of the test: Panel A reports the four
directive-specific coefficients from spec (c), and Panel B places each
directive on the 2008--2020 timeline by adoption year and transposition

\clearpage\section{9. Discussion and
Limitations}\label{discussion-and-limitations}

Cumulative net restrictive policy change for all-migrant-worker reforms
is positively associated with the high-skilled share of
employment-related permits in the denominator-weighted bilateral panel,
after controlling for skill-targeted policy paths. The result survives
the unweighted bilateral comparison, corridor fixed effects, the no-UK
ILOSTAT control source, the work-visa-only narrowing, and
EU-directive-window controls. The aggregate and annual-flow bilateral
models do not produce it.

At the same time, the evidence does not support a strong claim that
restrictive policy changes increase the absolute number of high-skilled
workers. The PPML volume test in Section 7.3 returns a coefficient that
is not statistically different from zero. The pattern across the three
specifications is therefore compositional rather than volume-based: the
high-skilled share changes, but there is no clear evidence of an
increase in the absolute high-skilled permit count.

The thesis is designed to show whether observed policy changes are
systematically related to the skill composition of employment-related
permits, after accounting for destination and origin-year fixed effects
and the available controls. It is not designed to estimate the full
welfare effect of migration policy, the complete decision process of
governments, or the causal response of individual migrants to a specific
legal reform. The policy implications that follow from the bilateral
weighted result are developed separately in Section 10.

The first limitation is that statistical significance remains model
dependent. The denominator-weighted bilateral model is defensible
because the dependent variable is a share measured with different levels
of precision across permit cells, and because the specification absorbs
origin-year push factors. Because the weights are realized current
permit denominators, however, the preferred estimand gives more
influence to larger observed corridors and should not be read as an
equal-cell causal effect; the unweighted comparison checks how dependent
the finding is on this choice. The evidence is not equally strong
across all models. The unweighted bilateral comparison is weaker, the
aggregate all-migrant-worker-only models are not significant, and the
UK-inclusive weighted robustness check is not significant. Multiple
testing is also a concern because several specifications are estimated
on the same panel. The design partly addresses this concern by treating
the denominator-weighted bilateral model as the preferred specification
on theoretical grounds. The preferred result is also checked with
wild-cluster bootstrap inference. For these reasons, the result is best
described as evidence of a conditional, denominator-weighted composition
association, not as a settled causal effect.

A second limitation is the possible feedback between economic conditions
and policy choice. If governments liberalize because employers already
face shortages, a positive association between policy openness and
high-skilled entry may partly reflect underlying labor demand rather
than policy alone. If governments tighten policy during downturns, the
high-skilled share may rise because lower-skilled employment permits
fall more sharply, not because restrictions attract additional
high-skilled workers. The lagged unemployment and GDP-growth controls,
destination fixed effects, and origin-year fixed effects reduce this
concern, but they cannot rule out all anticipatory policy changes or
unobserved economic pressures. The EU27 placebo is relevant to this
concern. Because national labor-migration rules do not apply to EU
citizens in the same way as to third-country nationals, its positive and
marginal coefficient is consistent with destination-wide shocks,
including unobserved cyclical changes in permit composition, that may
affect both groups. The placebo does not identify the source of those
shocks, but it reinforces the need to interpret the estimated
association as conditional rather than as evidence of a fully isolated
policy effect.

A related political limitation is that immigration policy is shaped by
more than macroeconomic indicators. Public opinion, electoral
competition, coalition politics, employer lobbying, union pressure,
administrative capacity, and media salience can all affect when
governments tighten or liberalize labor-migration rules. This is
consistent with work showing that immigration preferences are shaped by
labor-market position and welfare-state concerns (Facchini and Mayda
2009). These political forces are difficult to measure consistently
across the full country-year panel. They are also not fully captured by
unemployment, GDP growth, fixed effects, or origin-year controls. If
political pressure both changes policy and affects the composition of
permits issued, part of the estimated association may reflect unobserved
political dynamics rather than the independent effect of restrictiveness
itself.

Two methodological extensions follow from these limitations. First,
fractional-response models are formally suitable for shares bounded on
{[}0, 1{]}. In this dataset, however, they are less transparent than the
denominator-weighted linear specification and do not change the
substantive interpretation. They are therefore retained as a robustness
check rather than used as the preferred estimator. Second, the
high-skilled migration literature emphasizes migrant networks, wage
premia, origin unemployment, quality-of-life indicators, and
origin-destination migration costs as important controls. The available
data do not contain a complete, harmonized set of these controls for the
post-2008 European panel. For this reason, the bilateral origin-year
fixed-effects design is preferable to adding incomplete controls only to
lower a reported p-value. Both extensions are productive directions for
future work.

\section{10. Policy Implications}\label{policy-implications}

The weighted bilateral result has three policy implications. They
concern the compositional association between general labor-migration
reforms and the skill mix of permits after skill-targeted policy changes
are held constant, the design of dual-track migration systems, and the
methodological lesson from the UK robustness exercise.

First, net restrictive change in general labor-migration policy is not
compositionally neutral. The estimated shift is consistent with
lower-skilled inflows falling relatively more, but the count results
cannot establish whether it reflects fewer lower-skilled permits, more
high-skilled permits, or both. General all-migrant-worker reforms, such
as tighter work-permit conditions, more demanding employer obligations,
lower or stricter quotas, and narrower access to rights attached to
legal entry and stay, are associated with a higher skill ratio among
permits issued in the following year. Because the preferred model uses
permit-denominator weights, the estimate places more influence on larger
permit corridors than on very small cells where the ratio can change
sharply after only a few permits. From a policy-design perspective, this
means that a reform package described as across-the-board may still be
associated with a higher high-skilled share in substantial
employment-permit flows.

Second, the result is relevant for the design of dual-track migration
systems. Parallel national schemes, such as Germany\textquotesingle s
Skilled Immigration Act 2020, France\textquotesingle s Talent Passport,
and the Dutch Highly Skilled Migrant scheme, operate in the same broader
institutional space. The preferred coefficient remains positive after
controlling for reforms aimed specifically at skilled/high-skilled
workers, which means the result should not be read simply as the direct
effect of those targeted routes.

Third, the UK robustness exercise offers a methodological lesson.
DEMIG-QuantMig records individual policy events, while Brexit was a
regime-level rupture in UK labor-migration policy. This mismatch helps
explain why the UK-inclusive estimates are treated as fragile. It also
suggests that future work should model treaty-level breaks separately,
rather than assuming that all major institutional changes can be
captured as ordinary policy events.

\section{11. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

This thesis asks whether labor-migration policy restrictiveness is
associated with the skill composition of long-term employment-related
immigration in European destinations between 2008 and 2020. The
aggregate country-year models do not provide strong evidence that
general all-migrant-worker restrictions change the high-skilled share.
By contrast, the improved preferred denominator-weighted bilateral model
with origin-year fixed effects and skill-targeted policy controls shows
a statistically significant positive association between cumulative net
restrictive policy change for all-migrant-worker reforms and the
high-skilled share of employment-related first residence permits. The
work-visa-only narrowing further strengthens this association.

In substantive terms, the preferred estimate implies that a one-unit
increase in the lagged cumulative net policy-change score for
all-migrant-worker reforms is associated with roughly a
0.33-percentage-point increase in the high-skilled share of
employment-related permits, holding high-skilled and low-skilled
target-group policy changes constant. The synchronized aggregate
cumulative and annual-flow models do not reach five-percent significance
under this same preferred design. The conclusion is therefore based on
the denominator-weighted bilateral model, not on the aggregate
policy-flow evidence.

The central conclusion is that larger cumulative net restrictive policy
changes for all-migrant-worker reforms are associated with a higher
skill ratio among employment-related permit inflows when the bilateral
model accounts for origin-year pressures, denominator precision, and
targeted high- and low-skill policy paths. The evidence does not support
a statistically significant increase in the absolute number of
high-skilled workers at the five-percent level. Future work should
extend the panel beyond 2020, add harmonized bilateral controls such as
migrant networks and wage differentials, and use reform-specific designs
in which individual policy changes generate sharper causal variation.

A cautious policy implication follows from these findings. The
regressions estimate the association between general all-migrant-worker
restrictiveness and permit composition while holding observed
skill-targeted policy changes constant; they do not estimate the
separate causal effect of maintaining an EU Blue Card or another
dedicated high-skill route. The following interpretation is therefore
mechanism-based rather than a direct evaluation of those schemes. Within
that limitation, the bilateral pattern is consistent with the view that
dedicated high-skill channels may help preserve the skill composition of
substantial employment-permit flows when general work-permit rules
become more restrictive. Policy makers should not treat this result as
evidence that any particular talent scheme causes that outcome, but it
does suggest that general and skill-targeted labor-migration policies
should be designed as connected parts of the same system.
